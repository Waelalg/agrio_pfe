\section*{Context}

Water availability is one of the main constraints on contemporary agriculture. Population growth, climate variability, irregular rainfall, soil degradation, and competition between domestic, industrial, and agricultural uses increase pressure on freshwater resources. Irrigation remains essential for stabilizing crop production, especially in regions where rainfall is insufficient or badly distributed during the growing season. At the same time, poorly controlled irrigation can waste water, increase pumping cost, leach nutrients, and expose plants either to water stress or excess moisture.

The irrigation problem is therefore not limited to supplying water. It requires deciding when water should be applied, how much should be applied, and how the decision should react to soil, plant, weather, and equipment conditions. Traditional irrigation practices often rely on fixed calendars, visual inspection, or operator experience. These approaches are useful in many practical contexts, but they can be insufficient when field conditions change rapidly or when water resources must be managed with higher precision.

\section*{Scientific Motivation}

Smart irrigation is a major application of precision agriculture because it connects agronomic decision-making with sensing, communication, data processing, and automation. Original prototype studies show that even low-cost systems can combine soil moisture sensing, microcontrollers, MQTT communication, and pump status monitoring to support irrigation control \cite{kodali2017mqttirrigation}. Other experimental works demonstrate that agricultural monitoring platforms can use field sensors to follow temperature, soil humidity, atmospheric pressure, solar radiation, and related variables needed by farmers to reason about irrigation \cite{balaceanu2019libelium}.

Machine learning and Digital Twin approaches extend this logic. Sensor data can be processed to classify irrigation status, forecast water needs, simulate alternative actions, or generate recommendations. Digital Twin studies in vineyards and commercial tomato farms show how sensor data, climate information, remote sensing, and simulation models can support irrigation scheduling under real agricultural conditions \cite{bellvert2023vineyarddt,millan2025tomatodt}. Reinforcement learning and interoperable Digital Twin architectures also indicate that future irrigation systems may combine simulation, optimization, and standardized data integration \cite{elhachimi2025collectiveirrigationdt,kallenberg2025interoperabledt}.

\section*{Research Problem}

Despite these advances, many irrigation systems remain partial. Some focus mainly on sensing and remote monitoring. Others implement automatic threshold control without predictive reasoning. Some machine-learning studies evaluate algorithms without integrating them into a complete operational workflow. Conversely, field-validated Digital Twin systems may depend on specialized infrastructure or agronomic calibration that is difficult to reproduce in a student engineering context. Another limitation is explainability: an operator should understand why a recommendation is produced before a pump command is validated.

The research problem addressed in this thesis is the following: how can an intelligent irrigation approach be studied, designed, implemented, and validated by combining the concepts of smart irrigation, IoT sensing, machine learning, Digital Twins, explainable multi-agent decision support, and supervised automation?

\section*{Objectives}

The general objective of this Engineering Diploma thesis is to study and develop an intelligent irrigation approach informed by original research on smart irrigation and agricultural Digital Twins. This objective is divided into the following sub-objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item analyze the scientific background of precision agriculture, smart irrigation, IoT communication, machine learning, and Digital Twins;
    \item compare original research prototypes and case studies related to irrigation monitoring, prediction, simulation, scheduling, and optimization;
    \item identify the functional and technical requirements of an intelligent irrigation contribution;
    \item design and implement the proposed contribution in the dedicated design and implementation chapters, including an explainable Multi-Agent System (MAS) decision layer;
    \item validate the implemented workflow technically and discuss its limitations with respect to field-validated research systems.
\end{itemize}

The developed contribution is intentionally introduced only at a high level in this introduction. Its architecture, implementation choices, interfaces, and validation evidence are presented later in the thesis, after the theoretical and state-of-the-art chapters. In addition to the Digital Twin, ML inference, simulation, and supervised control workflow, the contribution includes an explainable MAS service that combines specialized evidence from Digital Twin state, ML prediction, weather context, agronomic text notes, water-balance estimation, and safety rules. This MAS is an engineering decision-support layer and not a computer-vision system.

\section*{Methodology}

The thesis follows a progressive methodology. First, the background chapters establish the main concepts needed to understand intelligent irrigation: soil and weather variables, sensing, communication, decision support, machine learning, multi-agent reasoning, and Digital Twin functions. Second, the related-work study focuses on original research papers, case studies, prototypes, and experimental systems rather than review papers. Third, the comparison identifies what existing systems cover and where practical integration gaps remain. Fourth, the design and implementation chapters present the proposed contribution, including the MAS evidence-fusion layer. Finally, the validation chapter evaluates the technical behavior of the implemented workflow and defines the remaining agronomic and operational limitations.

\section*{Thesis Organization}

The thesis is organized as follows. The front matter contains acknowledgements, abstracts, and lists. The General Introduction presents the context, problem, motivation, objectives, methodology, and organization. Chapter 1 introduces the theoretical background of precision agriculture, smart irrigation, IoT, MQTT, machine learning, and Digital Twins. Chapter 2 reviews original research works on IoT-based irrigation, predictive irrigation, agricultural Digital Twins, and reinforcement-learning-based optimization. Chapter 3 merges the scientific positioning and comparative analysis of selected original papers. The following chapters present requirements, architecture, implementation, Digital Twin and machine-learning components, the MAS decision-support service, interfaces, deployment, validation, and conclusion.
